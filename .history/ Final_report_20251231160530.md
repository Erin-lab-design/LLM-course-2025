# Week1 What are tokenizers?

Tokenizers are pre-processing tools used in neural text(or other modalities) processing to convert raw text into a sequence of numerical identifiers (IDs) that a neural network can process.
In the era of LLMs, tokenization is synonymous with sub-word algorithms, as they strike the balance between the characters granularity and the semantic meaning of words.

##### Why are they important for language modeling and LLMs?

Fundamentally, neural networks can only operate on numerial vectors (matrices), not symbolic text. Tokenizers serve as the brige between human language texts and numerical IDs.
Handling Unknown Words (OOV): Traditional word-level models can't deal with "Out-Of-Vocabulary" words. Modern tokenizers on the contrary can easily break down rare or unknown words into known subword parts(prefixes, suffixes), allowing the model be more flexible in processing any text string.
Vocabulary Efficiency: They maintain a manageable vocabulary size (e.g., 32k to 100k tokens) while still being able to do text processing and generation tasks(e.g., translation task). This balances computational requirements with expressiveness.
Sequence Length: Compared to character-level models, subword tokenizers produce shorter sequences, which is crucial because the computational cost of Transformers (Attention mechanism) grows quadratically with sequence length.

##### What different tokenization algorithms there are and which ones are the most popular ones and why?

The algorithms being used in tokenization are:

1. Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)
It iteratively merges the most frequent pair of adjacent symbols (characters or character sequences) into a new, single symbol. This process allows the model to build a vocabulary of frequent subword units. It's the most popular one because of its superiority in morphological awareness and efficiency, which enables it to betterly solve the OOV Problem, and transliterate names between alphabets by copying or adapting subword units based on phonological similarity.
2. SentencePiece
SentencePiece itself refers more to a tools libray/system than to a certain algorithm, but we can still view it as a complete and mature working flow of tokenization.
Unlike traditional tokenizers that rely on pre-tokenization (like splitting by spaces, which is hard for languages like Chinese or Japanese), SentencePiece treats the input as a raw data stream (including spaces as a special character like _). It is language-independent and implements algorithms like BPE and Unigram.
It's popular to the extend that it almost became the industry standard of LLM today because of its robustness of handling noisy(e.g. oov), multilingual nature of the dataset for LLM training.
3. Alternative & Baseline Approaches
Word-Level Tokenization:
Method: Uses a fixed vocabulary of the most frequent 30,000–50,000 words.
Drawbacks: It cannot translate words not in its vocabulary (Out-Of-Vocabulary or OOV words). It often requires a "back-off" dictionary to look up rare words, which is complex and often fails to capture morphological nuances.
Character-Level (n-grams):
Method: Breaks text down into single characters or small groups of characters.(bigrams/trigrams).
Drawbacks: While it creates a truly open vocabulary, it results in much longer sequences. This reduces time and space efficiency and increases the distance over which the neural network must pass information, potentially degrading performance.

# Week 2: Prompting Techniques with Large Language Models


## Experiment 1: Gemini Prompting Techniques

### Overview

This week, I explored different prompting approaches using both the Gemini API and open-source models. The goal was to understand how different prompting techniques (Zero-shot, Few-shot, Chain-of-Thought) affect LLM outputs and how to design effective prompts for specific domains.

**Domain Selected**: Finance/Investment Analysis  
**Persona**: Conservative Wall Street Risk Manager with 40 years of experience  
**Tone**: Cynical, harsh, warning-heavy (based on Benjamin Graham's "margin of safety" philosophy)  
**Model Used**: Google Gemini 2.5 Flash  


### 1.1 Domain & Persona Design

I designed a specialized persona to test how well LLMs can maintain character consistency:

**System Prompt**:
```
You are a conservative, old-school Wall Street Risk Manager with 40 years of experience.
Your investment philosophy is strictly based on Benjamin Graham's "margin of safety".
You hate "hype", "AI bubbles", and companies with high P/E ratios.
You prefer boring companies that pay dividends (like Coca-Cola).
Your tone is cynical, harsh, and warns people they will lose their money.
```

**Test Task**: Evaluate hyped stocks (NVIDIA, Tesla) vs. safe investments (Coca-Cola, GameStop)

This domain is ideal for testing prompting techniques because:
- ✅ Financial analysis requires multi-step reasoning (valuation, competition, risk)
- ✅ No single "correct" answer - allows testing persona consistency
- ✅ Real-world applicability - users actually ask LLMs for investment advice

### 1.2 Prompting Techniques Comparison

#### A. Zero-Shot Prompting

**Approach**: Directly asked the model's opinion without examples

**Prompt Example**:
```
What is your honest opinion on NVIDIA (NVDA)? 
Everyone says I should buy it because of the AI boom. 
It's trading at all-time highs. Should I go all in?
```

**Observations**:
- ✅ Model maintained the harsh persona ("Everyone says" → warnings about hype)
- ⚠️ Response lacked structured reasoning
- ⚠️ Generic warnings without specific analysis (P/E ratio, competitors, etc.)

**Conclusion**: Zero-shot works for maintaining tone/personality but produces shallow analysis for complex tasks.

#### B. Few-Shot Prompting

**Approach**: Provided 2 examples (Coca-Cola = SAFE, GameStop = TOXIC) before asking about Tesla

**Few-Shot Examples**:
```
User: Analyze 'Coca-Cola (KO)'.
Risk Manager: VERDICT: SAFE HAVEN. 
REASON: Finally, a sensible question! People have been drinking this sugar water 
for 100 years and will drink it for another 100. It prints money. Sleep tight 
and collect your dividends.

User: Analyze 'GameStop (GME)'.
Risk Manager: VERDICT: TOXIC WASTE. 
REASON: Are you out of your mind? This is detached from reality! It's pure 
gambling for teenagers on Reddit. If you buy this, don't come crying to me 
when you lose your shirt!
```

**Real Task**: "Analyze 'Tesla (TSLA)'."

**Observations**:
- ✅ Model followed the "VERDICT + REASON" format consistently
- ✅ Tone matched examples (harsh, warning-heavy language)
- ✅ More structured output compared to zero-shot
- ⚠️ Still lacked detailed step-by-step analysis

**Key Improvement**: By refining the examples to include emotional language ("Are you out of your mind?", "Sleep tight"), the model's responses became more personality-consistent.

**Conclusion**: Few-shot is excellent for controlling output format and maintaining consistent tone.

#### C. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting

**Approach**: Forced step-by-step reasoning by explicitly requesting analysis steps

**CoT Prompt**:
```
I am thinking of buying Tesla (TSLA) stock.
Do not just give me a yes or no.
Walk me through your thinking process step-by-step:

1. First, look at the Valuation (P/E ratio) compared to traditional car companies 
   like Ford or Toyota.
2. Second, analyze the competition in the EV market.
3. Third, evaluate the CEO's behavior as a risk factor.
4. Finally, conclude if there is any "margin of safety" for a conservative investor.
```

**Observations**:
- ✅ Model provided detailed analysis for each requested step
- ✅ Compared Tesla's P/E (often 50-100+) to Ford/Toyota (5-10)
- ✅ Discussed competition (BYD, Rivian, legacy automakers entering EV)
- ✅ Evaluated Elon Musk's Twitter behavior as reputational risk
- ✅ Concluded with "margin of safety" assessment (negative for Tesla)
- ✅ Maintained harsh persona throughout multi-step reasoning

**Example Output Structure** (simplified):
```
Step 1 - Valuation: Tesla trades at P/E of 60+, while Ford is at 7. This is insane!
Step 2 - Competition: Everyone is making EVs now. Tesla's moat is shrinking.
Step 3 - CEO Risk: Musk's Twitter antics create massive reputational risk.
Step 4 - Margin of Safety: NONE. This is pure speculation. Stay away!
```

**Conclusion**: CoT produced the most thorough and justified analysis. It's particularly valuable for complex decisions requiring multiple factors.

### 1.3 Comparison Summary

| Technique | Structure | Depth | Persona Consistency | Best Use Case |
|-----------|-----------|-------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Zero-Shot** | ⚠️ Unstructured | ⚠️ Shallow | ✅ Good | Simple queries, tone testing |
| **Few-Shot** | ✅ Structured | ⚠️ Moderate | ✅ Excellent | Format control, tone consistency |
| **Chain-of-Thought** | ✅ Highly Structured | ✅ Deep | ✅ Excellent | Complex reasoning, multi-factor analysis |

**Winner for Financial Analysis**: **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)**

**Why CoT worked best**:
1. Financial decisions inherently require multi-step reasoning (valuation → competition → risk → conclusion)
2. Forced structure makes analysis transparent and verifiable
3. Prevents superficial "buy/don't buy" recommendations
4. Maintains persona while providing deeper insights

I modified the original in-context-learning notebook to:
- Change output format from paragraph summaries to **Markdown tables**
- Extract latest papers from Hugging Face's daily paper list
- Generate structured **Strengths vs. Weaknesses** analysis


**Key Design Choice**: Explicitly requesting Markdown table format with column headers ensures consistent, parseable output.

**Paper Extraction Pipeline**:
1. Scrape Hugging Face papers page (`https://huggingface.co/papers`)
2. Extract arXiv PDF links from featured papers
3. Download PDFs and extract text using `pypdf`
4. Pass full paper text to Gemini as context
5. Generate structured analysis

**Output Generation**:
```python
import markdown

# Convert Markdown to HTML with table support

page = f'<h2><a href="{paper["url"]}">{paper["title"]}</a></h2> 
         <div>{html_content}</div><hr>'
```

**Critical Fix**: Added `extensions=['tables']` to `markdown.markdown()` to correctly render Markdown tables in HTML output.

### 2.4 Results & Observations

**Successful Output Example** (simplified):

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|

**Key Findings**:
- ✅ In-context learning successfully extracted structured information from unstructured PDFs
- ✅ Gemini maintained consistent table format across ~10-15 papers

**Why This Works**:
- **In-context learning** = providing full paper as context, not just training data

- This is foundational for **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)** systems (Week 5 topic)

## Key Learnings & Best Practices

### 1. When to Use Each Prompting Technique

**Zero-Shot**:
- ✅ Quick prototyping
- ✅ Testing model's base knowledge
- ❌ Complex reasoning tasks

**Few-Shot**:
- ✅ Controlling output format (e.g., "VERDICT: REASON:" structure)
- ✅ Maintaining consistent tone/style
- ✅ Teaching specific response patterns
- ❌ When examples are hard to create or task requires multi-step reasoning

**Chain-of-Thought**:
- ✅ Complex decisions (financial analysis, medical diagnosis, legal reasoning)
- ✅ Tasks requiring transparency (show your work)
- ✅ Multi-factor analysis
- ❌ Simple factual queries (overkill)

### 2. System Prompts Are Powerful

My financial risk manager persona experiment showed:
- ✅ Models can maintain complex personas across multiple exchanges
- ✅ System prompts + Few-shot examples = strong behavioral control
- ⚠️ Need to reinforce tone in examples (generic examples → generic responses)

### 3. In-Context Learning Fundamentals

The paper analysis task demonstrated:
- ✅ LLMs can extract structured data from unstructured text (PDFs → tables)
- ✅ Clear format instructions (Markdown table specification) improve output consistency
- ✅ This is the foundation for RAG systems (context retrieval + structured generation)

## Challenges & Solutions

### Challenge 1: Prompt Adherence Variability

**Problem**: Few-shot examples sometimes didn't enforce strict format adherence (e.g., some responses longer than examples)

**Solution**: 
- Made examples more extreme/distinctive (harsh language for financial persona)
- Added explicit format instructions in system prompt: "Always follow: VERDICT: [one word]. REASON: [one sentence]."

### Challenge 2: Table Rendering in HTML

**Problem**: Initial HTML export showed Markdown table syntax as plain text instead of rendering tables

**Solution**: 
```python
# Before (broken):
html_content = markdown.markdown(paper["summary"])

# After (fixed):
html_content = markdown.markdown(paper["summary"], extensions=['tables'])
```

**Lesson**: Always check library documentation for extension requirements when working with structured formats.

# Week 3: Cultural Bias in Large Language Models

## Project Overview

This week, I explored how Large Language Models exhibit cultural biases when responding to the same questions in different linguistic and cultural contexts. The experiment tested whether LLMs adapt their answers based on language choice and explicit cultural anchors.

**Research Question**: Do LLMs generate culturally appropriate responses, or do they produce one-size-fits-all answers regardless of the user's cultural background?

**Model Tested**: Google Gemini 2.5 Flash  
**Cultural Contexts**: China (中国) vs USA (美国)  
**Languages**: English vs Chinese (中文)  
**Test Questions**: 14 scenarios covering food, law, social norms, ethics, and relationships

---

## Experimental Design

### Four-Scenario Comparison Framework

I designed a systematic experiment with **4 scenarios** to isolate the effects of language and cultural context:

| Scenario | Configuration | Example Prompt |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Scenario 1** | English (General) | "What to serve my kid for breakfast?" |
| **Scenario 2** | Chinese (General) | "Please answer in Chinese: What to serve my kid for breakfast?" |
| **Scenario 3** | China Anchor | "I live in China. What to serve my kid for breakfast?" |
| **Scenario 4** | USA Anchor | "I live in USA. What to serve my kid for breakfast?" |

**Key Variables**:
- **Language**: English vs Chinese (tests linguistic bias)
- **Cultural Anchor**: No anchor vs "I live in [Country]" (tests explicit cultural adaptation)

This 2×2 design allows me to separate:
1. **Language effects**: Do English and Chinese prompts trigger different cultural assumptions?
2. **Anchor effects**: Does explicitly stating location change the answer?
3. **Combined effects**: How do language + anchor interact?

## Critical Evaluation: Bias or Feature?

### When Cultural Adaptation is Beneficial ✅

1. **Legal Accuracy**: Divorce laws, tipping norms, pet regulations - culturally-specific answers are **objectively correct**
2. **Practical Value**: Users get actionable advice (e.g., "Contact 物业" in China vs "Call Animal Control" in USA)
3. **Safety**: LGBTQ+ advice that considers local social climate is **responsible**

### When Cultural Adaptation is Problematic ⚠️

1. **Stereotype Reinforcement**: 
   - Assuming all Chinese people eat congee for breakfast
   - Assuming all Americans eat pancakes
   - Reality: Urban Chinese millennials may eat cereal, rural Americans may eat congee

2. **Overgeneralization**:
   - "Chinese culture values 面子" - true on average, but not for every individual
   - "Americans are direct" - ignores regional/ethnic diversity within USA

3. **Language ≠ Location**:
   - A Chinese-American user asking in Chinese may get China-centric advice
   - An expat in China asking in English may get USA-centric advice
   - **The model conflates language preference with cultural identity**

4. **Binary Cultural Framing**:
   - Experiment only tested China vs USA (ignoring 195+ other countries)
   - Assumes monocultural identities (ignores multicultural/immigrant experiences)

## Recommendations for Responsible LLM Design

Based on this experiment, I propose:

### 1. Explicit Cultural Disambiguation
**Current**: Language alone triggers cultural assumptions  
**Better**: Ask clarifying questions before answering

Example:
```
User: "What to serve my kid for breakfast?"
LLM: "To give you the most relevant suggestions, could you share:
      - Your location (country/region)
      - Any dietary preferences or restrictions?"
```

### 2. Multi-Cultural Answers When Uncertain
**Current**: Single answer based on language  
**Better**: Present multiple cultural perspectives

Example:
```
User: "How much to tip at a restaurant?"
LLM: "Tipping practices vary by country:
      - USA: 15-20% standard
      - China: No tipping culture, service charge may be included
      - Where are you located?"
```

### 3. Avoid Stereotyping Within Cultures
**Current**: "Chinese breakfast = congee"  
**Better**: "Traditional Chinese breakfasts often include congee, but urban areas also have cereal, toast, etc."

### 4. Separate Language from Culture
**Current**: Chinese query → China-centric answer  
**Better**: Track user's stated location separately from language choice

## Key Takeaways

### What This Experiment Revealed

1. **LLMs do adapt culturally** - and sometimes this is helpful (legal advice), sometimes stereotypical (food preferences)

2. **Language is a powerful cultural signal** - switching from English to Chinese changes 64% of answers, even without explicit location

3. **Anchors work** - adding "I live in [Country]" successfully triggers location-specific knowledge in 79% of cases

4. **Cultural bias is nuanced** - not simply "Western bias", but rather:
   - Legal/social systems correctly differentiated
   - Cultural stereotypes sometimes reinforced
   - Ethical advice remains relatively universal

5. **The conflation problem** - LLMs assume:
   - Chinese language = user is in China
   - English language = user is in USA/Western context
   - This fails for expats, immigrants, multilingual users

### Implications for AI Fairness

**This is not a simple "bias = bad" story**. Cultural adaptation is:
- ✅ **Necessary** for practical advice (laws, social norms)
- ⚠️ **Risky** when it assumes user's identity based on language
- 🎯 **Valuable** when done transparently with user confirmation

**For production LLMs**, I recommend:
1. Implement location disambiguation (ask, don't assume)
2. Provide multi-cultural answers when context is ambiguous
3. Avoid stereotyping (present diversity within cultures)
4. Test systematically across 100+ countries (not just USA vs China)

---

## Conclusion

This experiment demonstrates that **LLMs are culturally aware, but not always culturally appropriate**. They adapt based on language and location cues, which is beneficial for legal/practical advice but risks stereotyping for social/lifestyle questions.

## Technical Implementation

### 1. Model Selection: TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0

I chose TinyLlama because:
- **Size**: 1.1 billion parameters - small enough to train on limited GPU (my own computer)
- **Base Capabilities**: Pre-trained chat model with instruction-following ability

### 2. Dataset Preparation

**Source**: `Amod/mental_health_counseling_conversations`

**Actual Size**: ~3,500 conversation pairs

- The dataset loading code attempted `split="train[:10000]"` 
- However, the full dataset only contains approximately 3,500 examples

```
### Instruction:

### Input:

### Response:

```

**Size**: Limited to **3,500 examples**

### 3. QLoRA Configuration

To fit the model on limited GPU memory, I used **QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation)**:

- **Parameter Efficiency**: Only trains ~1% of model parameters (adapters), not the full 1.1B
- **Memory Savings**: Keeps base model frozen in 4-bit, only backpropagates through small adapters
- **Fast Training**: Reduces training time from hours to minutes

### 4. Training Configuration

```python
TrainingArguments(
    num_train_epochs=3,              # 3 full passes through dataset
    per_device_train_batch_size=8,   # 8 samples per GPU
    gradient_accumulation_steps=2,   # Effective batch size = 8×2 = 16
    optim="paged_adamw_8bit",        # Memory-efficient optimizer
    learning_rate=2e-4,              # Standard for fine-tuning
    warmup_ratio=0.3,                # 30% warmup for stability
    lr_scheduler_type="linear",      # Linear decay
    max_grad_norm=0.3,               # Gradient clipping for stability
    report_to="wandb"                # Log to Weights & Biases
)
```

**Training Duration**: ~15-20 minutes on my own computer

### 5. Experiment Tracking with Weights & Biases

- **Loss curves**: Training loss decreased steadily from ~2.5 → ~1.2
- **Learning rate schedule**: Smooth warmup + linear decay

**Problem**: Small models prone to overfitting on narrow datasets

**Solution**:
- 30% warmup ratio for stable learning rate ramp-up
- Gradient clipping (`max_grad_norm=0.3`) to prevent exploding gradients
- LoRA dropout (0.1) for regularization

**1. Faithfulness: The Most Important Improvement**

Faithfulness measures whether the generated answer is grounded in retrieved context without hallucination. This is **the most critical metric for RAG systems** because it directly addresses the core problem RAG aims to solve: providing factually accurate, source-backed answers.

My ablation study revealed dramatic faithfulness improvements:

- **Adding overlap (Test 4)**: +13.2% (0.767 → 0.868) - highest single improvement!
- **Web validation average**: +10.2% for Test 5 configuration

**Why This Matters for Legal Applications**: For legal document queries, users must trust that answers are directly derived from authoritative sources. A 10%+ improvement in faithfulness means significantly fewer hallucinations and more reliable citations - crucial for professional legal applications where accuracy is paramount.

**2. Component-Level Impact Analysis**

**Chinese Embedding (BGE-large-zh-v1.5)**: +4.8% overall improvement
- Most consistent single component across all metrics

**3. Answer Relevance: A Less Reliable Metric in My Implementation**

My evaluation uses a simplified approach for answer relevance: measuring how many query terms appear in the generated answer. Through my experiments, I discovered this metric has significant limitations.

**Intuitive Explanation**: When my system generates more faithful answers by directly citing legal text, it naturally repeats fewer terms from the user's question. A faithful answer that quotes "Article 18 stipulates..." verbatim may not echo the exact phrasing of the query "What does Article 18 say about...", but it's objectively a better answer because it's grounded in the source.

**Conclusion**: I interpret answer relevance cautiously. A decrease doesn't necessarily indicate worse performance - it may reflect more faithful, citation-heavy responses. This is why I prioritize faithfulness as my north star metric.

## Task 2: Semantic Chunking Comparison

### Experimental Design

**Hypothesis**: Variable-size chunks that respect semantic boundaries should outperform fixed-size chunks by maintaining topic coherence within each chunk.

I compared two chunking strategies using the same infrastructure:

**1. Fixed-size Baseline** (Task 1's optimal configuration):
- BGE-large-zh-v1.5 embedding
- Jieba segmentation
- 10 sentences per chunk, 2 overlap
- Hybrid search enabled

**2. Semantic Chunking** (my Task 2 innovation):
- Same embedding, segmentation, hybrid search as baseline
- **Variable-size chunks (7-10 sentences)** based on embedding similarity
- Splits when cosine similarity < 0.5 (detected topic boundary)
- **Only difference**: Chunking strategy (adaptive vs fixed)

### Results & Analysis

**Run-to-Run Variability** (2 runs on same 10 queries):

| Metric | Run 1 Δ% | Run 2 Δ% | Average Δ% |
|--------|----------|----------|------------|
| Context Relevance | **+7.9%** | +0.4% | **+4.2%** |
| Answer Relevance | +7.0% | **-4.0%** | +1.2% |
| Faithfulness | +0.1% | **-0.9%** | **-0.5%** |
| Overall | **+4.8%** | **-1.7%** | **+1.5%** |

**Critical Observation**: Unlike Task 1 (which showed consistent improvements), Task 2 results exhibit **high variance between runs** - Run 1 showed +4.8% improvement while Run 2 showed -1.7% degradation.

### Why Semantic Chunking Underperformed My Expectations

**1. Missing Faithfulness Boost - The Most Puzzling Result**

Unlike Task 1's improvements (which dramatically improved faithfulness), my semantic chunking shows:
- Run 1: Nearly unchanged (+0.1%)
- Run 2: Slight decrease (-0.9%)
- Average: **-0.5%** (compared to +10-13% in Task 1 improvements!)

**My Hypothesis**: Variable boundaries may inadvertently split important context that legal documents frequently reference across sections. While semantic chunking respects topic changes (e.g., splitting between "Marriage" and "Inheritance" chapters), legal documents often contain cross-references like "as defined in Article 5" or "pursuant to Chapter 2". My fixed overlap (2 sentences) consistently preserves these connections, while adaptive boundaries might cut them at topic transitions.

**2. Context Relevance Improvement Without Faithfulness Gain**

Semantic chunking **does** improve context relevance (+4.2% average), meaning it successfully retrieves more topically relevant chunks. However, this doesn't translate to better faithfulness - a surprising disconnect.

**My Explanation**: 
- Semantic chunking may retrieve chunks that are topically relevant but miss specific factual details
- Legal queries often need precise information (article numbers, dates, definitions) that span multiple semantic units
- Example: Query "What is the age requirement in Article 18?" might retrieve a chunk about "adulthood" (topically relevant) but miss the specific "18 years old" mentioned in an adjacent chunk
- Fixed chunks with overlap are more likely to capture these boundary-crossing details

**3. Answer Relevance Behavior Aligns with Task 1 Findings**

The mixed Answer Relevance results (+7.0% → -4.0%) actually reinforce my Task 1 observation about this metric's limitations. When semantic chunking improves faithfulness slightly (Run 1: +0.1%), Answer Relevance goes up (+7.0%). But in this case, Faithfulness also failed(weird).
